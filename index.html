Research

I. Human Trajectory Prediction in Crowded Environments SFU · 2025–present

How can a robot predict where people will walk in crowded, human-centered spaces — while understanding how people react to each other?

Self-driving cars and service robots operate in human-centered environments: busy streets, campuses, shopping malls, hospitals, and offices, surrounded by people. Working safely there depends on anticipating human motion: a vehicle yielding to a pedestrian about to step off the curb, or a service robot weaving through a crowded corridor. Two properties largely decide whether a trajectory prediction method works in these real crowds: whether it generalizes to new scenes, and whether it is socially aware, capturing how people influence one another. Yet they are rarely studied together.

My work

- Intention as utility: confidence-based prediction of human intentions for social robot navigation (IEEE T-RO, under review).
- Real-world deployment: at MaRobot AI, I built a probabilistic human-prediction framework with online intention inference, deployed on ROS2 and parallelized with JAX.
- Patent: Methods and Systems for Predicting One or More Future States of an Entity (PCT application, pending).

The landscape: a survey along three axes

In my PhD depth report, Toward Generalizable and Socially-Aware Human Prediction: A Survey, I map the field along three axes:

Generalizability
Does the model transfer to unseen scenes? Leave-one-out evaluation, online adaptation after deployment, and Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL).
Social Awareness
How do agents influence each other? Physics rules, game theory, social pooling, attention — and interaction-annotated datasets.
Evaluation
Are we measuring the right thing? Displacement errors (ADE/FDE) versus calibration metrics that reflect real-world reliability.

Every surveyed method placed by how it generalizes (x-axis) and how it models social interaction (y-axis). The top-right corner is empty: no method uses IRL to recover the rewards behind human social interaction.

What's next

That empty corner is where I'm heading: learning the reward functions behind different types of social interaction with IRL, using recent interaction-annotated datasets such as JRDB-Social and TrajNet++ — toward human trajectory prediction that is both generalizable and socially aware.

II. Scalable Safety Guarantees: Fixing the Leaking Corner in HJ Reachability UCSD & SFU · 2022–2025

How can we compute safety guarantees for high-dimensional robots — fast, and without giving up correctness?

Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability computes the set of states from which a robot can reach a goal or avoid failure, together with the optimal control policy. It comes with strong guarantees, but its cost grows exponentially with the state dimension (the "curse of dimensionality"). Decomposing a system into self-contained subsystems makes the computation tractable, but it can produce wrong results — errors that appear at the corners of the decomposed sets, which we call the leaking corner issue. My work identifies why this happens and how to fix it.

1. Admissible Control Sets

- Cause: the leaking corner comes from inexact decomposition of the target set and inconsistent control policies across the coupled subsystems.
- Fix: an admissible control set enforces consistent control actions across subsystems, giving exact reachable sets and optimal policies for decomposable targets.
- Beyond exact decomposition: conservative under-approximations for inexactly decomposed targets, which a local update in the full-dimensional space can refine to the exact result.

Left: exact vs. inexact decomposition of the target set. Right: the backward reachable set from admissible control matches the true one, while plain subsystem decomposition does not.

2. Threshold Strategy

- Theory: a proof that the leaking corner issue occurs only at the corners of the decomposed sets.
- Algorithm: a local updating procedure that repairs just those corners, recovering the exact reachable set while keeping the speed of decomposed computation.

Left: why leaking can only happen at the corner. Right: the local updating process that recovers the exact reachable set.

III. 3D Catheter Reconstruction from Image Sequences UCSD · 2022

How can we recover the true 3D shape of a catheter from a low-resolution endoscopic camera?

Flexible catheters bend inside the body, and knowing their 3D shape is key to controlling them precisely. Instead of extra sensors, this project recovers the shape from the camera images alone, using a learning-based optimization.

My work

- Shape model: represented the catheter as a Bezier curve with 3 control points.
- Learning from images: built a loss function over the image sequence and used gradient descent in PyTorch to learn the control points, optimizing the reconstructed 3D shape toward the true one.
- Kinematics: derived the forward and inverse kinematics of a 4-actuator, constant-curvature catheter.

The reconstructed catheter shape is optimized frame by frame over the image sequence.

IV. Teleoperation for Catheter Control SJTU · 2021

How can a surgeon steer a flexible, cable-driven catheter as naturally as moving their own hand?

Continuum manipulators can bend into many shapes, which makes them valuable for minimally invasive surgery — but that flexibility also makes them hard to control by hand. This project builds a teleoperation system so the catheter can be driven intuitively with a haptic device.

My work

- Motion mapping: mapped the haptic device's motion to the motors that pull the driving cables, which set the manipulator's shape.
- System: implemented the full teleoperation pipeline in ROS using C++.
